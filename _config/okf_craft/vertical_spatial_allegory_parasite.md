# Vertical Spatial Allegory: The Architecture of Class Conflict

In *Parasite* (Bong Joon-ho), narrative structure is literally mapped onto **physical elevation and gravitational architecture**. In prose, using vertical space externalizes social hierarchies without heavy-handed exposition.

---

## 1. The Three Vertical Tiers

*   **The Upper Domain (The Glass Sanctuary):**
    *   *Spatial Traits:* High ceilings, sun-drenched gardens, floor-to-ceiling glass, pristine geometric sightlines.
    *   *Sensory Traits:* Fresh air, clean linen, unscented spaces. The wealthy are protected from weather and environmental physics.
*   **The Semi-Basement (The Precariat Transit):**
    *   *Spatial Traits:* Ground-level windows where pedestrians urinate, damp concrete, patchy Wi-Fi, constant street-level noise.
    *   *Sensory Traits:* Mildew, fried grease, subway sweat.
*   **The Sub-Basement (The Invisible Foundation):**
    *   *Spatial Traits:* Bunker beneath the pantry, subterranean darkness, zero natural light, total absence of legal existence.
    *   *The Thematic Shock:* The revelation that the struggle is not simply the poor vs. the rich—it is the desperate poor battling the even more desperate subterranean underclass to stay in the light.

---

## 2. Invariant Rules for Spatial Allegory in Prose

1. **Gravity Always Punishes the Bottom:**
   * Natural elements flow downward. In *Parasite*, the torrential rain that creates a "quaint, refreshing morning" for the rich family physically washes raw sewage into the semi-basement home of the poor family.
2. **The Ineradicable Scent Marker:**
   * Wealth and poverty carry biological/chemical signatures that crossing boundaries cannot disguise. In *Parasite*, it is "the smell of the subway / boiled rags" that betrays the family. In prose, use sensory olfactory markers as uncrossable caste lines.
