# Possible Worlds Theory & The Modal Discrepancy Matrix

In literary narratology and cognitive story design, narrative depth cannot be generated by flat external events or surface-level character descriptions. Marie-Laure Ryan’s **Possible-Worlds Theory (PWT)** models the storyverse not as a single linear timeline, but as a dynamic, multi-dimensional modal system.

At the core of the story sits the **Textual Actual World (TAW)**—the objective physical baseline of factual reality within the fictional construct. Surrounding this baseline is an orbit of **Alternative Possible Worlds (APWs)** continuously generated, projected, and renegotiated by the minds of the characters.

---

## 1. The Five Core Modal Worlds of Character Interiority

Every compelling character operates within five distinct modal spheres:

1.  **Knowledge Worlds ($K$-Worlds):**
    *   *Definition:* The character’s epistemic beliefs regarding what is true, false, possible, or impossible in the Textual Actual World.
    *   *Narrative Tension:* $K$-world divergence from the TAW creates dramatic irony, cognitive vulnerability, paranoia, and tragic miscalculations.
2.  **Wish Worlds ($W$-Worlds):**
    *   *Definition:* The axiological preferences, heartfelt desires, fantasies, and idealized outcomes the character longs to realize.
    *   *Narrative Tension:* The distance between a character’s $W$-world and their real-world baseline ($TAW$) is the fundamental fuel of motivation.
3.  **Obligation Worlds ($O$-Worlds):**
    *   *Definition:* The deontic network of moral laws, social contracts, sacred oaths, tribal taboos, and systemic duties the character internalizes.
    *   *Narrative Tension:* Clash between $W$-world (what I crave) and $O$-world (what duty commands) creates intense moral agonizing and guilt.
4.  **Intention Worlds ($I$-Worlds):**
    *   *Definition:* The tactical, operational action plans formulated by the character's ego to transform the actual world ($TAW$) into closer conformity with their desire ($W$-world).
    *   *Narrative Tension:* The failure or thwarting of an $I$-world forces an emergency reassessment and costlier replanning.
5.  **Fantasy Universes ($F$-Universes):**
    *   *Definition:* The oneiric dreams, hallucinations, counterfactual speculations, and self-authored fictions created within the character's imagination.

---

## 2. The Modal Discrepancy Matrix

A character’s psychological dimensionality is directly proportional to the structural friction between their modal worlds:

```
[TAW: Objective Story Reality] <------- Collision -------> [K-World: Flawed Beliefs]
                 ^                                                      ^
                 |                                                      |
[W-World: Raw Desire] <-------------- Collision -------------> [O-World: Moral Duty]
```

### The 4 Major Modal Conflicts:

*   **The Tragic Blindspot ($K\text{-world} \neq TAW$):** The protagonist acts rationally based on their knowledge, but their knowledge is objectively wrong (e.g., Oedipus killing his father; Othello believing Desdemona is unfaithful).
*   **The Moral Crucible ($W\text{-world} \perp O\text{-world}$):** What the character loves or hungers for requires violating their core ethical oath or societal law (e.g., Lancelot loving Guinevere; a cop protecting a guilty child).
*   **The Tactical Impasse ($I\text{-world} \not\rightarrow TAW$):** The character’s active plan collapses when reality resists their assumptions, forcing either a moral compromise or psychological surrender.
*   **The Paralyzing Double Bind ($O_1 \perp O_2$):** Two valid, inviolable duties demand mutually exclusive actions (e.g., duty to family vs. duty to the realm).

---

## 3. The Governing Principles of Fictional Worlds

*   **Principle of Minimal Departure:** Readers intuitively assume the fictional world matches the real physical world in all physical, biological, and psychological laws, except where the text explicitly mandates an alteration. Writers must never explain mundane physics; focus textual energy exclusively on the points of departure.
*   **Principle of Diversification:** Narrative interest and "tellability" are a direct function of the modal distance and conflict between character APWs and the TAW. If all characters know the truth ($K = TAW$), agree on values ($W = O$), and execute flawless plans ($I \implies TAW$), narrativity drops to zero.

---

## 4. Writer's Scene-Planning Diagnostic

Before writing a high-stakes scene, audit the focal character’s modal coordinates:
*   [ ] **What does this character mistakenly believe is true?** ($K$-world error)
*   [ ] **What do they desperately crave that they cannot openly state?** ($W$-world secret)
*   [ ] **What moral, social, or professional rule forbids their desire?** ($O$-world boundary)
*   [ ] **What physical action are they taking right now to bridge the gap?** ($I$-world execution)
